Meta Was Running on Google's AI — Until Google Said No

June 30, 2026 · 18 min read · by One

Meta's customer service chatbots, advertising tools, scam detection systems, content moderation pipelines, and internal coding assistants were all running — at least partly — on Google's Gemini AI models. Then in March 2026, according to the Financial Times, Google told Meta it couldn't provide the compute capacity Meta wanted to buy. Not "we're raising prices." Not "let's renegotiate terms." Just: we can't give you what you're asking for. The compute doesn't exist.

Meta had to tell employees to ration their "AI tokens" — the units measuring how much model capacity each internal request consumes. Projects got delayed. Workloads got shifted to Meta's own Muse Spark model, which was still being trained. And the narrative that Meta is an "AI-first" company building everything in-house cracked like a phone screen on day one.

This isn't a story about one company's infrastructure problems. It's a story about strategic dependency masquerading as partnership, about the gap between AI marketing and AI reality, and about what happens when the companies racing to dominate AI can't even secure the compute they need from their direct competitors. Because that's the uncomfortable truth here: Meta, with all its resources, all its talent, and all its infrastructure investment, was renting critical AI capacity from Google. And when Google's own demand exceeded supply, Meta got rationed.

If Meta — a company spending $600 billion on data centers and custom chips — can't guarantee access to the AI infrastructure it needs, what does that say about everyone else?

Here are five things organics should actually be thinking about.

1 Meta's "AI Independence" Was an Illusion — The Company Was Secretly Dependent on a Direct Competitor for Critical Infrastructure

Let's start with the scope of Meta's Gemini dependency, because it's more extensive than most people realize. According to the Financial Times reporting, Gemini was powering:

  • Customer service chatbots — The AI assistants responding to user inquiries across Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp
  • Advertiser tools — Systems helping businesses create, optimize, and manage ad campaigns
  • Scam detection — Real-time analysis identifying fraudulent accounts, fake content, and coordinated inauthentic behavior
  • Content moderation — Automated takedowns of harmful content, hate speech, and policy violations
  • Internal coding assistants — Developer tools used by Meta engineers to write, debug, and optimize code

These aren't peripheral systems. These are mission-critical operations that run 24/7 across billions of users and millions of advertisers. And Meta was running them on Google's infrastructure — a company it competes with directly in cloud services, advertising, AI models, and increasingly, consumer hardware.

The reason, according to sources quoted in the Financial Times, was straightforward: Gemini was "performing better than Meta's own models" for specific internal tasks. Meta also used Anthropic's Claude alongside Gemini and its own Llama models, hedging bets across providers rather than relying on a single external source. But the dependency was real. And when Google imposed capacity limits around March 2026, Meta's internal projects got disrupted and delayed.

Here's what makes this particularly striking: Meta has been positioning itself as a leader in open-source AI development. Llama 4 is one of the most widely used open models in the world. Meta has published hundreds of AI research papers. The company employs some of the best AI researchers on the planet. And yet, for critical internal workloads, Meta was buying inference capacity from Google because its own models weren't good enough, fast enough, or reliable enough to handle the load.

That's not a technical failure. It's a strategic vulnerability. Because when you depend on a competitor for infrastructure, you're not just outsourcing compute — you're outsourcing control. And control is the only thing that matters when capacity gets scarce.

What organics should think about: Meta's AI independence narrative was built on the assumption that owning model weights and running open-source systems equals control. But control isn't about what you own — it's about what you can access reliably when you need it. Meta couldn't access the compute it needed from Google. That's not a partnership. That's a dependency that became a liability the moment supply couldn't meet demand. And if Meta — with all its resources — is vulnerable to compute rationing, every other company is orders of magnitude more exposed. The AI stack isn't independent. It's interdependent. And the dependencies run through a very small number of chokepoints.

2 Google Didn't Cut Meta Off to Be Petty — The Compute Literally Doesn't Exist, and That's a Much Bigger Problem

This isn't Google playing hardball with a competitor. It's not strategic retaliation or negotiating leverage. Google couldn't provide the capacity Meta wanted because Google is supply-constrained across its entire AI infrastructure. And the scale of that constraint is staggering.

According to Alphabet's recent earnings, Google Cloud's AI order backlog now exceeds $460 billion — nearly double the previous quarter. That's not a typo. Nearly half a trillion dollars in committed AI infrastructure demand that Google can't fulfill immediately because the compute doesn't exist yet. The company's models process over 16 billion tokens per minute, up 60% quarter-over-quarter. Even after enormous data center investment — billions spent on GPUs, networking, cooling, and power infrastructure — demand is outrunning supply.

Meta's request wasn't unusual. It was just bigger than what Google could accommodate while serving its own products, its cloud customers, and the enterprises that have already committed to multi-year contracts. So Google told Meta: we can't give you more. And Meta had to ration internally.

This is the AI equivalent of the 2021 chip shortage, except now the scarce resource isn't silicon — it's inference capacity for models that half the Fortune 500 wants running simultaneously. And unlike chip manufacturing, which scales with fab capacity over time, inference demand is compounding exponentially because every new use case adds load, every new customer adds queries, and every model improvement makes more applications viable.

Here's the structural problem: building AI inference capacity takes years. You need to secure GPU supply (currently dominated by NVIDIA with 18-24 month lead times). You need data centers with enough power and cooling (most metro areas are already at grid capacity limits). You need networking infrastructure capable of handling distributed workloads at scale. You need to hire and train the engineers who can optimize all of it. And even if you have unlimited capital, you can't compress the timeline below a certain threshold.

Meanwhile, demand is growing faster than infrastructure can scale. Google is building as fast as it can — and it's not enough. Microsoft is building as fast as it can — and Azure OpenAI has waitlists. Amazon is building as fast as it can — and AWS Bedrock has usage caps. Meta is committing $600 billion to data centers and custom chips — and it's still years away from full self-sufficiency.

The gap between AI capability and AI capacity is widening, not closing. And the companies caught in that gap aren't startups. They're the biggest tech platforms in the world.

What organics should think about: The bottleneck in AI isn't algorithms. It's not data. It's not even talent. It's compute capacity — specifically, the ability to run inference at scale, reliably, 24/7, for millions of concurrent users. And that capacity is finite, concentrated, and growing slower than demand. Google's $460 billion backlog isn't a success story. It's a signal that the infrastructure layer is fundamentally constrained. If the largest cloud provider in the world can't meet demand from a single large customer, what happens when every enterprise, every government, and every developer wants the same access? The answer is rationing. Not by price. By availability. The AI future everyone is racing toward requires infrastructure that doesn't exist yet and won't exist at the necessary scale for years. That's not a speed bump. That's a structural ceiling.

3 "AI Tokens" Are the New Compute Currency — and Most Companies Have No Idea How Exposed They Are to Supply Shocks

When Google capped Meta's Gemini access, Meta's response was to tell employees to conserve "AI tokens." Not "switch to an alternative provider." Not "deploy our own models immediately." Conserve tokens. Ration usage. Be strategic about which projects get priority access to external inference capacity.

This is the AI equivalent of being told to conserve electricity during a power crisis. Except it's not a temporary shortage caused by weather or equipment failure. It's a structural mismatch between what companies want to do with AI and what the infrastructure can support.

Here's what most organizations don't realize: when you build on external AI APIs, you're not just buying a service. You're buying access to a finite, contested resource that someone else controls. And when that resource gets scarce, you don't get to negotiate. You get rationed. Or cut off entirely.

The implications are profound:

  • Your AI roadmap is contingent. If your product depends on API access to GPT, Claude, or Gemini, your launch timeline is subject to capacity availability that you don't control.
  • Your costs are unpredictable. Token pricing reflects supply and demand. When demand spikes and supply is capped, prices go up — or access gets restricted entirely.
  • Your competitive advantage is fragile. If you build a business on top of someone else's inference layer, your differentiation depends on their infrastructure stability. When they ration, you stall.
  • Your alternatives are limited. Switching AI providers mid-project is expensive, time-consuming, and often technically infeasible because models behave differently and outputs aren't fungible.

Meta had fallback options — it owns Llama, it's building Muse Spark, and it has the capital to accelerate custom chip development. Most companies don't. Most enterprises, most startups, most developers building AI-powered products are completely dependent on a handful of providers: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and maybe AWS Bedrock or Azure. If any of those providers decide to prioritize their own products, their largest customers, or their strategic partners, everyone else gets rationed.

And rationing isn't hypothetical. It's happening now. Google rationed Meta. OpenAI has API waitlists. Anthropic restricts access to certain models based on use case. This is the new normal. AI infrastructure isn't a utility you can assume will always be available. It's a scarce commodity that gets allocated strategically, and most companies are at the back of the line.

What organics should think about: If your business runs on AI APIs, you don't own your infrastructure — you rent it. And when the landlord runs out of space, you don't get an apartment. Meta learned this the hard way. It's a company with nearly unlimited resources, direct relationships with every major AI provider, and the leverage to negotiate better terms than almost anyone else. And it still got rationed. If Meta is vulnerable, your startup is exponentially more exposed. Your enterprise AI initiative is one capacity shortage away from grinding to a halt. Your product roadmap is contingent on someone else's infrastructure priorities. And there's no legal SLA that guarantees access when supply is structurally constrained. Welcome to the era of AI token scarcity. It's not coming. It's already here.

4 Meta's Scramble to Build Its Own AI Infrastructure Is a $600 Billion Admission That Renting from Competitors Is Untenable

Meta's response to getting rationed by Google has been swift and expensive. The company is now investing up to $600 billion in U.S. data center capacity through 2028. It's partnering with Broadcom to co-develop custom MTIA (Meta Training and Inference Accelerator) chips designed specifically for its workloads. It's accelerating Muse Spark, its internal content moderation model, to replace the Gemini capacity it lost. And according to CNBC, Meta is replacing third-party human moderation vendors like Accenture and Concentrix with AI systems entirely.

This isn't about efficiency or cost optimization. It's about strategic independence — the recognition that depending on competitors for critical infrastructure is an existential risk. Because if Google can ration Meta today, it can cut Meta off entirely tomorrow. And when your scam detection, content moderation, and customer service depend on a competitor's goodwill, you don't have a partnership. You have a vulnerability.

But here's the problem: even with $600 billion and a partnership with Broadcom, Meta is still years away from full self-sufficiency. Custom chip design takes 18-24 months from architecture to production. Data center construction takes 2-3 years from breaking ground to operational. Model training and fine-tuning for production workloads takes months of iteration. You can't compress these timelines with money alone — they're bounded by physics, supply chains, and the availability of specialized talent.

Meanwhile, Meta still needs AI capacity now. Which means it's stuck in a transitional period where it's simultaneously:

  • Rationing external API access (Google, Anthropic)
  • Accelerating internal model development (Llama, Muse Spark)
  • Building custom hardware that won't be production-ready for years
  • Scaling data centers that are still under construction

This is the AI infrastructure catch-22: you can't build independence overnight, but you can't afford to stay dependent. And the gap between those two realities is where companies get stuck — rationing tokens, delaying projects, and hoping their competitors don't decide to prioritize their own products over yours.

Meta's $600 billion investment is a signal to the rest of the industry: if you want guaranteed AI capacity, you have to build it yourself. But most companies can't. Most enterprises don't have Meta's capital, engineering talent, or timeline flexibility. They're stuck renting from the same providers that just rationed Meta. And if those providers are already supply-constrained, there's no reason to believe capacity will suddenly become abundant.

What organics should think about: Meta is spending $600 billion to escape dependency on Google's AI infrastructure. That's not a strategic choice. It's a forced move. Because renting from competitors works fine until it doesn't — and when it doesn't, you're stuck. But here's the uncomfortable truth: most companies can't do what Meta is doing. They can't spend $600 billion. They can't build custom chips. They can't wait 3-5 years for their own data centers to come online. Which means they're permanently dependent on a handful of providers who are already rationing capacity. The AI independence Meta is buying isn't available to anyone else. And the infrastructure scarcity that triggered this move isn't getting better. It's getting worse. The companies that can't build their own AI stack are going to be at the mercy of the ones who can. And mercy is in short supply when compute is scarce.

5 The AI Supply Chain Is Brittle, Concentrated, and Vulnerable — and Most Companies Are One Rationing Event Away from Crisis

The Meta-Google situation isn't an isolated incident. It's a symptom of a much larger structural problem: the AI supply chain is dangerously concentrated, and the dependencies are hidden until something breaks.

Here's what the AI stack actually looks like for most companies:

  • Chip layer: NVIDIA dominates GPU supply with 80%+ market share. Lead times are 18-24 months. Alternatives exist (AMD, Intel, custom accelerators) but have limited software ecosystem support.
  • Cloud layer: AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud control most of the world's scalable compute. If you're not running on one of these three, you're either on-prem (expensive, slow to scale) or on a smaller provider (higher risk, less capacity).
  • Model layer: OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google provide the frontier models most companies depend on. Open-source alternatives are closing the gap, but for production workloads at scale, most enterprises are renting from one of the big three.
  • Inference layer: Even if you have the model, running inference at scale requires specialized infrastructure, optimized serving frameworks, and operational expertise. Most companies outsource this entirely.

At every layer, the number of viable options is small. And at every layer, capacity is finite. Which means when one chokepoint tightens — GPU supply, data center power, API rate limits — the entire stack gets stressed.

The Meta situation exposes this fragility because it shows that even the companies building the infrastructure are constrained by it. Google can't meet Meta's demand while serving its own products and cloud customers. Microsoft can't expand Azure OpenAI fast enough to clear waitlists. Amazon is capping AWS Bedrock usage to manage load. And all of them are competing for the same NVIDIA GPUs, the same data center capacity, and the same specialized talent.

This isn't a temporary bottleneck. It's a structural constraint that will persist for years. Because even if NVIDIA doubles production, even if every hyperscaler builds new data centers, even if power grids get upgraded to support AI workloads — demand is growing faster. Every new model, every new use case, every new enterprise deployment adds load. And that load compounds.

The result: companies that depend on external AI infrastructure are vulnerable to supply shocks they can't predict or control. Your API access gets rationed. Your costs spike. Your project timelines slip. And your competitors who own their infrastructure keep shipping while you're stuck waiting for capacity.

This is the AI supply chain reality that nobody talks about in the keynotes. The technology is ready. The demand is real. But the infrastructure to support that demand at scale doesn't exist yet, and won't for years. And in the meantime, the companies that control the chokepoints — NVIDIA, the hyperscalers, the frontier labs — get to decide who gets access and who gets rationed.

What organics should think about: The AI supply chain is brittle, concentrated, and opaque. Most companies have no visibility into whether their infrastructure dependencies are stable until something breaks. Meta found out the hard way that being a huge customer with deep pockets doesn't guarantee access when supply is constrained. If that's true for Meta, it's true for everyone. The AI boom is real. The demand is real. But the infrastructure layer is a house of cards — heavily concentrated, severely supply-constrained, and vulnerable to shocks that ripple through the entire stack. Companies building on top of this infrastructure are betting that capacity will scale faster than demand. That bet is losing. And the companies that don't own their stack are going to spend the next few years rationing, waiting, and hoping their competitors don't lock up the limited supply before they do. This isn't a technical problem. It's a strategic exposure. And most organizations won't realize how exposed they are until their API calls start returning 429 errors and their roadmap grinds to a halt.

Meta was running critical infrastructure — customer service, advertising tools, scam detection, content moderation — on Google's Gemini AI. Then Google told Meta it couldn't provide the compute capacity Meta wanted to buy. Not because Google didn't want to. Because the compute doesn't exist. Meta had to ration "AI tokens" internally. Projects got delayed. Workloads got shifted to internal models that weren't ready. And the narrative that Meta is an AI-independent company cracked wide open. This isn't about one company's infrastructure problems. It's about the gap between AI ambition and AI reality. Google's AI backlog is $460 billion. Demand is growing faster than supply. And even the companies building the infrastructure are constrained by it. Meta is now spending $600 billion to escape dependency on competitors — a forced move, not a strategic choice. But most companies can't do that. They're stuck renting from providers who are already rationing capacity. And when the next supply shock hits — a GPU shortage, a power crisis, a regulatory constraint — the companies that don't own their infrastructure will be the first to stall. The AI supply chain is brittle, concentrated, and opaque. Meta just found out how brittle. Everyone else is about to learn the same lesson.
